## 0:00 — mystery (gancho) · A moon that should be dead

There is a small, frozen moon at Saturn that should be completely dead.
Instead, it is shooting fountains of water into space.
For more than a decade, a single machine flew through those fountains, tasted them, and sent back a message nobody expected.
Then NASA made a decision that sounds impossible: to end that machine's mission on purpose, before anything went wrong.
To understand why, we have to begin with the moon.

## 0:29 — moon (desenvolvimento) · Tiger stripes

William Herschel discovered Enceladus in 1789.
For two centuries it was only a faint dot of light, a moon about five hundred kilometers wide.
Its surface is covered with clean, bright ice, which reflects almost all of the sunlight that reaches it.
That should make it frozen and quiet.
But at its south pole, long cracks, nicknamed tiger stripes, cut through the ice.
From those cracks, jets of ice particles, water vapor and organic chemicals escape into space.
Cassini first saw the plumes shortly after it arrived at Saturn, and the discovery changed what the whole mission was about.

## 1:12 — journey (desenvolvimento) · Seven years to Saturn

Cassini left Earth on the fifteenth of October, 1997, on top of a Titan rocket.
It carried a European probe called Huygens, built for a single purpose: to fall through the atmosphere of Titan.
The trip took almost seven years, and along the way Cassini borrowed speed from Venus twice, then Earth, then Jupiter.
In July 2004, Cassini fired its main engine for ninety-six minutes, to slow down and let Saturn's gravity capture it.
It became the first spacecraft ever to orbit the planet.
The plan was four years of exploration.
It lasted thirteen.

## 1:54 — tasting (virada) · Tasting the spray

In 2008, Cassini flew only fifty kilometers above Enceladus, straight through the spray.
Its instruments did not just look at the plume.
They sniffed it.
What they tasted was not what anyone had predicted for a frozen moon.
The water was coming from a global ocean hidden beneath the ice.

## 2:17 — chemistry (virada) · What the ocean is made of

Two instruments did the tasting.
One was a mass spectrometer, which weighs the molecules in the gas.
The other was a dust analyzer, which examines the ice grains one by one.
More than ninety percent of the gas was water vapor.
The rest included carbon dioxide, methane, and simple organic molecules, in amounts higher than scientists expected.
The ice grains carried salt, and also tiny grains of silica, the stuff of sand.
Silica like that forms where hot water, above about ninety degrees Celsius, meets rock.
So the ocean is not only there.
Its floor is warm, with hot water flowing through the rock.
In October 2015, Cassini made its deepest pass through the plume, skimming just forty-nine kilometers above the ice, and its instruments registered molecular hydrogen, roughly one percent of the gas.
On Earth, hydrogen like that comes from hot water reacting with rock on the seafloor, and whole ecosystems around hydrothermal vents live off that chemical energy.

## 3:26 — ingredients (desenvolvimento) · Three ingredients

Scientists often boil the search for life down to three ingredients.
Liquid water.
Chemical building blocks.
And a source of energy.
On Enceladus, Cassini found signs of all three.
That does not mean there is life.
It means there is a place where life would have a chance.
And that, more than anything else, is what turned a quiet moon into the most important target Cassini had left.
Everything that followed was built around that one question.

## 4:02 — system (desenvolvimento) · A whole system of worlds

While the plumes made the headlines, Cassini kept working across the whole Saturn system.
It flew past dark, battered moons, and it watched a storm wrap around the entire planet.
It mapped the rings, and the six-sided jet stream circling the north pole.
In thirteen years it completed nearly three hundred orbits, and made one hundred sixty-two targeted flybys of moons.
And almost every one of those orbits was steered by the gravity of a single moon: giant Titan.

## 4:33 — webb (desenvolvimento) · Still watching

The story did not end with Cassini.
In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope took a first look at Enceladus.
It found that the plume of water stretches more than twenty times the size of the moon itself.
The fountains that Cassini discovered are so powerful that they feed the entire Saturn system.

## 4:56 — titan (virada) · A second ocean world

Cassini's other great discovery came from the probe it carried.
On the fourteenth of January, 2005, Huygens parachuted into the haze of Titan.
For about three and a half hours it was in radio contact with Cassini, and it captured about three thousand five hundred images.
This is what they look like, assembled into a time-lapse of the descent.
Bright highlands cut by what look like drainage channels, and dark plains below.
But Titan's rivers do not carry water.
At about minus one hundred eighty degrees Celsius, it is methane that falls as rain and flows.
Cassini's radar later found lakes and seas of liquid methane.
Two ocean worlds, around a single planet.

## 5:45 — dilemma (virada) · The problem with success

By 2017, Cassini had outlived every plan.
It was healthy, but almost out of the fuel it used to steer.
A spacecraft that cannot steer cannot be told where to go.
And Cassini had been built on Earth, where microbes are everywhere.
If it ever crashed into Enceladus, it could contaminate the very ocean we most wanted to study.
Scientists call this planetary protection: a rule that guards other worlds, and also the integrity of our own search for life.
So the safest ending was to send Cassini into Saturn itself, where it would burn up completely.
But NASA did not simply switch it off.
It chose one last act of exploration.

## 6:32 — dive (desenvolvimento) · Twenty-two dives

The final chapter began with a goodbye to Titan, on the twenty-second of April, 2017.
That last flyby used Titan's gravity to tilt Cassini's orbit so that it threaded the gap between Saturn and its innermost ring.
Nobody had ever sent a spacecraft through that gap.
It is roughly two thousand kilometers across, and dust there could damage the spacecraft, so the dish antenna pointed ahead like a shield.
Over the next five months Cassini passed through the gap twenty-two times, measuring Saturn's gravity and magnetic field from closer than ever before.
On the last five passes it brushed the outermost layer of the atmosphere and sampled Saturn's air directly, for the first time.
Those measurements showed that the rings weigh less than expected, and that they are slowly raining onto the planet.

## 7:25 — lastday (virada) · September 15, 2017

Hours before the end, Cassini took its final picture of the place where it would enter the atmosphere.
Then it turned its antenna toward Earth, and began sending its science data live.
As the air thickened, the thrusters fought to keep the dish pointed home.
Cassini was falling at more than seventy thousand miles an hour.
At about fifteen hundred kilometers above the clouds, the atmosphere began to win.
The spacecraft started to tumble, and the signal stopped.
Saturn is so far away that radio waves need eighty-three minutes to reach Earth.
At 11:55 Universal Time, the engineers on Earth saw the line go flat.
By then, Cassini had already been gone for eighty-three minutes.

## 8:14 — legacy (conclusao) · The question left behind

Cassini sent back more than four hundred fifty thousand images and over six hundred gigabytes of data.
It found ocean worlds, seas of methane, and rings that may be only a few hundred million years old.
If you enjoy missions told like this, subscribe, because the next story is already in production.
And Cassini left behind a question that is still open: is anything alive in the ocean of Enceladus?
NASA's Dragonfly is planned to fly on Titan, and Europa Clipper is already on its way to the ocean moon of Jupiter.
Cassini did not find life.
It found a map of where to look.
And it made sure that when we get there, those oceans will still be untouched.
