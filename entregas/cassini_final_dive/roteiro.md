On the fifteenth of September, 2017, in a control room in California, a room full of engineers waited for a signal to stop.
The machine they were listening to had worked perfectly for twenty years.
It had crossed the Solar System, dropped a probe onto another world, and shown us oceans where nobody expected to find them.

The story really starts in 1980, when Voyager One flew past Titan and could not see through its haze.
Scientists wanted to go back, and stay.
That is how Cassini was born.
On the fifteenth of October, 1997, a Titan rocket lifted off from Florida carrying two spacecraft stacked as one.

## 1:39 — arrival (desenvolvimento) · Arrival at Saturn

On the first of July, 2004, Cassini faced the most dangerous minutes of its life.
To enter orbit, it had to turn around and fire its main engine for ninety-six minutes.
If the burn stopped early, Saturn's gravity would fling the spacecraft away, and seven years of travel would be wasted.
It worked.
Cassini became the first human-made object ever to orbit Saturn.
On the way in, it crossed the ring plane through a gap between two of the rings.
A few months later, it captured the most detailed portrait of Saturn ever made: one hundred twenty-six images stitched together.

## 2:19 — huygens (virada) · Landing on Titan

On Christmas Day, 2004, Cassini released Huygens, and the little probe began a three-week fall toward Titan.
Titan is Saturn's largest moon, wrapped in an orange haze so thick that nobody had ever seen its surface.
On the fourteenth of January, 2005, Huygens plunged into that haze, hanging from a parachute.
No one knew whether it would land on rock, on mud, or in a sea.
It landed, and it kept transmitting, sending back pictures from the surface of a world in the outer Solar System.
It remains the only landing ever made there.

## 2:58 — titan (desenvolvimento) · A world with rain

What Huygens saw on the way down was a landscape that looked strangely familiar.
Bright highlands, cut by what look like river channels, and dark plains below.
On the ground, the probe bounced, slid and wobbled for about ten seconds before it stopped.
It left a hole twelve centimeters deep, and scientists concluded that the ground was soft, something like damp sand.
The rivers, however, were not made of water.
On Titan, it is so cold that water behaves like rock.
Instead, methane falls as rain, flows in rivers, and gathers in lakes and seas.
Titan is the only other world in the Solar System with liquid on its surface.

## 3:42 — enceladus (virada) · The moon that sprays water

Then, almost by accident, Cassini found something that changed the mission.
Near the south pole of Enceladus, a small, bright moon, jets of ice were shooting into space.
The plumes came from cracks in the ice, and they carried water from below.
In 2008, Cassini flew within fifty kilometers of the surface, straight through the spray, and tasted it.
Later measurements showed that the water comes from a global ocean hidden under the ice.
In 2015, Cassini dived through the plume only forty-nine kilometers up and detected molecular hydrogen, about one percent of the gas.
On Earth, hydrogen like that is produced where hot water meets rock on the ocean floor, a possible source of energy for living things.
Nobody is saying there is life there.
But Enceladus had become one of the best places in the Solar System to look.

## 4:39 — saturn (desenvolvimento) · A world worth a lifetime

Meanwhile, on Titan, radar revealed rivers, lakes and seas, not of water, but of liquid methane.
Around Saturn's north pole, a six-sided jet stream circled the planet.
A record-setting storm wrapped the whole planet, and Cassini watched it grow.
And on the nineteenth of July, 2013, Cassini slipped into Saturn's shadow and turned around.
In that picture, a pale blue speck hangs far beyond the rings: Earth, and everyone who has ever lived.
Four years later, Cassini would photograph us again from even farther away, as a single point of light between the rings.

## 5:18 — reasons (virada) · Why destroy it?

By 2017, Cassini was healthy.
But it was almost out of fuel.
Fuel is what lets a spacecraft steer.
Without it, the team could no longer control where Cassini would go.
A spacecraft drifting without control could one day hit Enceladus or Titan.
And Cassini had flown from Earth.
NASA could not take the risk that microbes riding on it might one day reach those oceans.
Contaminating Enceladus would ruin the search for the very thing we wanted to find.
So the plan was settled years before: Cassini would end its life in Saturn's atmosphere, where it would burn up.
But NASA decided not to waste the final months.

## 6:06 — finale (desenvolvimento) · The Grand Finale

On the twenty-second of April, 2017, Cassini flew by Titan for the last time.
The pull of that moon bent its orbit into a path no spacecraft had ever flown.
For five months, Cassini would dive, again and again, through the gap between Saturn and its rings.
That gap is about two thousand kilometers wide, and no one knew exactly what was inside it.
So the team turned the big antenna into a shield.
It made twenty-two dives.
On each one, it measured Saturn's gravity and magnetic field more closely than ever before.
In the last five, it skimmed the top of the atmosphere itself, tasting the air of the planet.

## 6:51 — finale_science (virada) · What the dives found

The dives answered questions that scientists had argued about for decades.
Cassini measured Saturn's rings from the inside, and weighed them with the tug of their gravity.
They turned out to be lighter than many expected, with less than half the mass of the small moon Mimas.
A ring that light looks clean and young, perhaps no more than a few hundred million years old, though scientists still debate it.
Cassini also found that the rings are raining down on Saturn: ice and dust pour onto the planet at a surprising rate.
And for the first time, an instrument tasted the upper atmosphere directly, including molecules that came from the rings.
Saturn's rings are not permanent.
We happen to live while they are still here.

## 7:39 — lastday (virada) · The last hours

On the thirteenth of September, Cassini sent back some of its last pictures: Saturn's rings, in sunlight, from a million kilometers away.
Its final image shows the night side of the planet, at the exact place where the spacecraft would enter the atmosphere, hours later.
Then Cassini turned its antenna toward Earth and spent its final hours sending data.
On the morning of the fifteenth, it began to enter Saturn's atmosphere.
At first, the air was so thin that Cassini's thrusters could hold it steady, with its antenna pointed home.
But the atmosphere grew thicker.
Cassini was falling at more than seventy thousand miles an hour, over a hundred thousand kilometers an hour.
The thrusters pushed back harder and harder, until they could do no more.
Then the spacecraft began to tumble, its antenna lost Earth, and the signal stopped.

## 9:13 — legacy (conclusao) · What Cassini left behind

Cassini traveled almost five billion miles.
It completed nearly three hundred orbits of Saturn.
It returned more than four hundred fifty thousand images, and over six hundred gigabytes of science data.
Scientists have used that data to write nearly four thousand papers, and they are still writing.
It found two oceans where we expected none, one under the ice of Enceladus and one inside Titan.
It found seas on Titan, and hundreds of lakes.
And it left behind a question: is anything living in those oceans?
NASA's Dragonfly rotorcraft is planned to fly on Titan, and Europa Clipper is already on its way to a moon of Jupiter with an ocean of its own.
Cassini will not answer that question.
But it is the reason we know how to ask it.
